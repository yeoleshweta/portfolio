Case study · John Deere Financial · Jan–Aug 2021

When did ‘better’ actually mean better?

A new web version of ABTools, a credit tool used all day by John Deere Financial analysts, looked cleaner. I ran a two-wave study with the same four power users to find out whether it was actually easier to use, and where it still got in the way.

The short version

Problem

A redesign that looks newer isn’t proof it works better, especially for experts with up to 20 years of habits.

My role

UX Researcher. Planned the study with the JDF product team, ran both waves, analysed and presented the results.

Prologue

A UX team people liked, but didn’t yet rely on for evidence

While this study was running, the JDF UX team surveyed 40 product team members about working with UX (June 28 to July 6, 2021). The answers showed a gap this study could help close.

35 of 40 had worked with a UX practitioner, and 27 were very satisfied.

Top asks: more dedicated UX resources (22) and UX coaching (14).

liked for mindset… rarely for evidence. This study had to show the numbers.

Chapter 01 · The problem

Experts don’t forgive a pretty slowdown

Credit analysts make same-day decisions, sometimes in a 15-minute review, while taking 10 to 20 calls a day. Some had used the desktop ABTools for over 20 years. A post-launch survey alone couldn’t tell real improvement apart from novelty or change aversion.

The questions I set out to answer

  1. Did usability actually improve across the six dimensions the team cared about?
  2. Which dimensions improved most, and which barely moved?
  3. Where does friction remain in the daily workflow?

Chapter 02 · The plan

Measure the same people before and after

I planned the study with the JDF product team inside our usual discover → define → develop → deliver process. The key choice was timing: capture a baseline before launch, then wait until people had really used the new tool.

Design decisions, and what each one cost

Decision 1

Within-subjects

Why: each person is their own comparison, so role and tenure don’t muddy the change.

Cost: fixed order. The old tool was always rated first.

Decision 2

Familiarity window

Why: lets novelty and change aversion fade before wave 2.

Cost: other things change over months, too.

Decision 3

One power user per role

Why: depth and full role coverage from people who live in the tool.

Cost: four people can’t be generalised.

Decision 4

SUS + custom dimensions

Why: SUS gives a benchmark; six JDF dimensions show where it changed.

Cost: the custom scale isn’t validated.

Decision 5

% favourable reporting

Why: easy for leadership to read at a glance.

Cost: hides the spread of answers.

Decision 6

Interviews alongside scores

Why: scores say how much; open probes say why.

Cost: one coder, no second check.

Chapter 04 · The workflow

Following one credit decision from queue to handoff

I mapped what participants told me onto their daily workflow. Most remaining friction clustered around reading history and moving through queues.

Chapter 05 · What the numbers said

Usability went up. Satisfaction barely moved.

SUS rose from below every benchmark to above all of them. Every dimension improved, but by very different amounts. With four people, these are descriptive results, not statistical proof.

Chapter 06 · What people said

Chapter 07 · Design deep dive

The Notes problem: formatting is information

Analysts typed comments with careful spacing so others could scan them quickly. The new tool squeezed every note into one paragraph, in a small box, with text that didn’t grow when they zoomed.

“The comments are all together. I typed in very well layout with spaces, but it shows us as a big chunk. It is hard to read.”

Participant, wave 2

Chapter 08 · The verdict

What the redesign got right, and what it missed

Keep (4)

  • Same tab layout and labels as the old tool, so experts needed no retraining
  • Unused fields removed
  • Moved to the web: faster and stable
  • Simpler error messages

continuity is a feature for experts

Fix (10)

  • Notes display and zoom
  • Overlays that close only with X
  • Errors with no next step
  • Split queues and lost queue position
  • Removed route-to-bottom option
  • A “don’t touch” cursor on a loan number people need to copy
  • Buttons that fail silently
  • No default comment type
  • Employee search that misses the user
  • FileNet in a separate browser tab

Chapter 09 · From findings to patterns

Fix it once, in the design system

Instead of a list of bugs, I framed the findings as six reusable patterns, so other JDF tools wouldn’t repeat them. Fig 07 on this canvas shows each one as a before/after sketch.

Comment display

Keeps line breaks and spacing; expands in place.

Relative type

All text in rem, so it scales with browser zoom.

Modal standard

Close on outside click or Esc; side panel when people need to compare.

One error pattern

What happened, why, and what to do next.

List patterns

Inclusive search, kept queue state, pin, refresh and export.

Clear states

Disabled and read-only look and behave clearly; nothing fails silently.

Chapter 10 · Impact

Evidence, not just a launch

01

Leadership got proof

A benchmarked before/after result instead of “users seem to like it”.

02

Notes went to the top

The Notes issue became a high-priority backlog item.

03

Research kept going

I recommended a feedback loop, behavioural analytics and research before each release.

Where it led

Later, the UX team’s FY24 objective was to “pave the path for Elite Product UX”. Two of its strategic initiatives were defining UX metrics and building research operations. Studies like this one were the kind of evidence those goals asked for.

Chapter 11 · Looking back

What I’d keep, and what I’d add

Limitations

  • Four participants, no control group
  • The old tool was always rated first
  • I was embedded with the team, which may bias answers
  • Self-reported attitudes, not observed behaviour
  • One coder for the themes

Next time

  • A diary study during the familiarity window
  • Task metrics: time on task, error rate, SEQ
  • Severity ratings for every issue, not just frequency

What this taught me

  • Timing is a method decision
  • Small samples give depth, not proof
  • The most useful finding was qualitative
  • For experts, continuity is part of good design
